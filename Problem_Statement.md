An Emotion-Aware Patient Feedback System for AI-Assisted GP Consultations

The adoption of artificial intelligence in primary care is accelerating. GP practices across the UK and internationally are deploying AI tools to assist with symptom triage, clinical note summarisation, diagnostic suggestion, and appointment prioritisation. For clinicians, these tools represent a genuine improvement in efficiency and decision support. For patients — particularly older adults, those with limited digital literacy, and those already anxious about their health — they represent something far more unsettling: a machine they did not choose, do not understand, and were not fully consulted about, making contributions to decisions that affect their bodies and lives.

The problem does not end when the patient leaves the consulting room. It deepens in the waiting area and at home, where unanswered questions compound into distrust. Standard post-appointment feedback mechanisms — paper forms, brief survey links, numeric satisfaction ratings — are structurally incapable of capturing this distrust. They ask patients to rate their experience on a scale, not to articulate a fear. They record what a patient selects, not what they actually feel. And critically, they offer no mechanism for the patient to ask back. The result is a growing and largely invisible population of patients who leave AI-assisted appointments carrying unresolved concerns about data privacy, about who made their diagnosis, about whether a computer can be wrong, and about whether anyone will listen if they say so — concerns that erode trust in their GP practice, reduce adherence to clinical advice, and accelerate disengagement from primary care entirely.

This project addresses that gap directly. We propose EmpathyCheck, an emotion-aware post-appointment patient feedback system designed specifically for older and non-technical adults returning from GP consultations in which AI was used in any form. The system begins with a three-screen patient-facing interface — accessible via a waiting room kiosk or a personalised link sent by the GP practice — that greets patients by name, explains the process in plain language, and invites them to choose between typed and spoken responses before a single question is asked. The questionnaire itself is short, jargon-free, and built around large tap targets and high-contrast design principles drawn from WCAG 2.1 AA accessibility standards.

The core technical contribution is a two-signal emotion detection layer that operates entirely on-device, without transmitting biometric data of any kind. The first signal is a lightweight facial expression model running in the browser via face-api.js, which produces a continuous emotional valence score from the patient's webcam feed. The second signal is derived from interaction heuristics — dwell time on individual answer options, re-selection frequency, and hesitation intervals — which serve as a secondary channel for inferring emotional state in patients who decline camera access or use audio mode. When a patient selects a positive response — such as "I feel fine about the AI" — but their combined emotion signal falls below a calibrated threshold indicating discomfort, the system flags a mismatch. Rather than accepting the surface-level response, the system surfaces a gentle amber alert and triggers a large language model prompt that explains, in plain and reassuring terms, precisely what role the AI played in that patient's consultation, why their concern is understandable, and what protections are in place.

The LLM integration is the final and most consequential layer of the system. At the close of the questionnaire, before submission, every patient is offered an open input — available in both text and audio formats — through which they may ask any question about their appointment, about AI in healthcare, or about how their data is used. The language model responds not with generic information but with a reply dynamically conditioned on the patient's detected emotional state throughout the session: warmer and more reassuring for patients whose emotion signals indicated sustained anxiety, more informative and direct for those who appeared comfortable. This approach recognises that what an anxious 74-year-old needs to hear about data privacy is structurally different from what a curious 45-year-old wants to know, even if they ask the same question in the same words.

The GP-facing side of EmpathyCheck is a subscription dashboard through which practices generate personalised feedback links, monitor response volumes, review anonymised mismatch alerts, and track the questions their patient population is asking most frequently. This last capability is of particular clinical value: it surfaces, in aggregate, the specific fears and misconceptions that a practice's communication strategy has failed to address, enabling targeted improvements to how AI is introduced and explained during consultations.

The target population is adults aged 60 and over attending NHS-affiliated and private GP practices in the United Kingdom, a cohort that is both disproportionately affected by the emotional dimensions of AI-assisted care and disproportionately underserved by existing digital feedback infrastructure. A pilot deployment across two contrasting practices — one urban with a younger demographic skew, one semi-rural with a predominantly older patient list — would enable direct comparison of mismatch rates, question submission rates, and pre-to-post-session comfort score shifts across patient populations. By treating emotional state as a first-class input rather than an afterthought, and by giving patients a genuine mechanism to ask back, EmpathyCheck aims to convert post-appointment anxiety into post-appointment understanding — and in doing so, to rebuild the trust between patients and the AI-assisted care that is increasingly treating them.
